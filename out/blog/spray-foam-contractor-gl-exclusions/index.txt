28:T1ad0,<h1>Why Your Standard Contractor GL Policy Won't Cover Spray Foam Claims</h1>
<p>Most spray foam contractors assume their general liability policy covers them on every job. The certificate looks fine. The premium gets paid. Then a homeowner calls about foam that expanded into their HVAC system, or a tenant in a commercial building develops respiratory symptoms two weeks after application — and the claim comes back denied.</p>
<p>Here's why that happens, and what you can do about it before it costs you.</p>
<h2>The Chemical Application Exclusion</h2>
<p>Standard contractor general liability policies are written for the broad contractor universe — framers, electricians, plumbers, roofers. They're not designed around a trade that involves spraying two reactive chemicals that undergo an exothermic reaction and emit volatile organic compounds on every job.</p>
<p>Most policies contain what's called an <strong>"exclusion for products that are incorporated into the work"</strong> or a <strong>pollutant exclusion</strong> that effectively removes coverage for chemical releases during or after application. When an underwriter sees spray foam or polyurethane insulation on the application, they may include:</p>
<ul>
<li>Exclusions for "chemical application" or "chemical installation"</li>
<li>Reduced sub-limits for completed operations claims arising from the work product itself</li>
<li>Blanket pollution exclusions that treat VOC off-gassing as a pollutant event</li>
</ul>
<p>The result: the GL policy that cost you $4,000 per year pays zero on a $35,000 property damage claim because the foam you applied is classified as a "product" and your "product" caused the damage.</p>
<h2>What "Completed Operations" Actually Means for Foam Contractors</h2>
<p>"Completed operations" coverage extends your GL beyond the jobsite and beyond the period of work. It covers claims that arise after you've finished the job and moved on.</p>
<p>For spray foam contractors, completed operations is where most of the real risk lives:</p>
<ul>
<li>A homeowner calls six months after the job saying foam expansion cracked drywall</li>
<li>A commercial tenant reports headaches and attributes them to foam off-gassing from the insulation you applied in the space below</li>
<li>A building inspector finds that foam was applied over electrical wiring in a way that creates a fire hazard</li>
</ul>
<p>All of these are completed-operations claims. If your GL policy has an exclusion for chemical application or reduced limits for completed ops on foam products, you're exposed on every job you've ever done.</p>
<h2>The Pollution Exclusion Problem</h2>
<p>Here's where it gets worse. Many spray foam claims involve a third-party alleging exposure to chemicals — VOCs, MDI isocyanates, blowing agents, or other compounds released during application or curing.</p>
<p>Under the standard ISO GL form, there's a <strong>pollution exclusion</strong> that eliminates coverage for:</p>
<blockquote>"Bodily injury or property damage which would not have occurred in whole or part but for the actual, alleged, or threatened discharge, dispersal, seepage, migration, release or escape of 'pollutants' at any time."</blockquote>
<p>VOCs and isocyanates are routinely classified as pollutants by insurance carriers. Which means a tenant who claims they were harmed by fumes from your work isn't covered under your standard GL — that's a pollution claim, and the pollution exclusion kicks it out.</p>
<p>This is why spray foam contractors need <strong>Contractor Pollution Liability (CPL)</strong> in addition to GL — not instead of it. CPL is the policy written specifically to cover the chemical exposure claims that standard GL excludes.</p>
<h2>The Off-Ratio Gap Nobody Talks About</h2>
<p>There's another exclusion that hits spray foam contractors harder than almost any other trade: the <strong>defective product or defective work exclusion</strong>.</p>
<p>When spray foam is applied off-ratio — meaning the A-component (isocyanate) and B-component (polyol) aren't mixed at the correct ratio — the foam doesn't cure correctly. It can:</p>
<ul>
<li>Remain tacky and continue to off-gas incompletely reacted isocyanate</li>
<li>Lose its structural properties, requiring complete removal</li>
<li>Cause property damage as uncured foam expands differently than specified</li>
</ul>
<p>An off-ratio event is simultaneously a <strong>defective product claim</strong> and a <strong>pollution claim</strong>. Standard GL policies typically exclude both. The result is a claim with no coverage at all — from a policy the contractor assumed would protect them.</p>
<h2>What a Spray Foam-Specific GL Policy Actually Covers</h2>
<p>A GL policy placed by a specialist who understands spray foam should include:</p>
<p><strong>1. Chemical application coverage</strong> — explicitly endorsing the work instead of excluding it.</p>
<p><strong>2. Completed operations with realistic limits</strong> — often $2M aggregate, sized to the real cost of foam removal and remediation.</p>
<p><strong>3. A pollution exclusion with a carve-back</strong> — or a separate CPL policy that coordinates with the GL to cover what the main policy excludes.</p>
<p><strong>4. Products coverage for the foam materials</strong> — covering claims that trace back to the material as applied, not just the underlying chemical.</p>
<p><strong>5. Off-ratio endorsement or rider</strong> — addressing defective-work claims that arise from ratio errors.</p>
<p>None of these are standard features of a generic contractor GL. They have to be specifically placed with markets that write spray foam accounts.</p>
<h2>How to Know If You Have a Coverage Problem</h2>
<p>Read your current GL policy's exclusions section. Look for:</p>
<ul>
<li>Any exclusion mentioning "chemical" or "chemical application"</li>
<li>The pollution exclusion — and whether it's absolute or contains a contractor exception</li>
<li>Any sub-limit on completed operations that's lower than your per-occurrence limit</li>
<li>Language excluding "your product" or "your work" from property damage coverage</li>
</ul>
<p>If you find any of these — or if you don't know where to look — call us. We review existing policies as part of every quote conversation, and we'll tell you exactly where your coverage ends.</p>
<h2>The Bottom Line</h2>
<p>A spray foam contractor with a generic GL policy isn't uninsured. They're underinsured in ways that only become visible at claim time. And in this trade, the most common claims — completed-operations property damage, chemical exposure, off-ratio events — are often the exact claims those exclusions were written to deny.</p>
