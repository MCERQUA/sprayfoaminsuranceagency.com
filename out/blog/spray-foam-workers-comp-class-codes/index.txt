28:T2354,<h1>Spray Foam Workers' Comp: Class Codes, Chemical Risk, and Why Misclassification Costs You</h1>
<p>Workers' compensation for spray foam contractors is one of the most frequently mishandled policies in the trade. Brokers who don't understand the work classify applicators under generic insulation codes, painting codes, or even general labor — producing premiums that look fine until the audit, and coverage that may not respond correctly when a chemical-exposure injury happens.</p>
<p>Here's what correct workers' comp looks like for a spray foam operation, why it matters, and what to look for when you're reviewing your program.</p>
<h2>The Injury Profile of Spray Foam Work</h2>
<p>Before getting into class codes, it helps to understand what kinds of injuries spray foam applicators actually experience. The injury profile is different from general construction in ways that affect how insurance responds.</p>
<p><strong>Chemical inhalation injuries</strong> — exposure to MDI isocyanates, VOCs, and blowing agents during application. Acute exposures cause respiratory irritation; chronic or high-dose exposures can cause occupational asthma and isocyanate sensitization. These are the injuries most likely to be disputed if the policy isn't structured correctly.</p>
<p><strong>Fall injuries</strong> — spray foam applicators work at heights: roofs, attics, crawlspaces, and elevated commercial spaces. Fall injuries from ladders and rooflines are common and often severe.</p>
<p><strong>Burn injuries</strong> — the exothermic reaction of spray foam produces significant heat. Contact burns from the foam itself and heat injuries from the equipment are occupational hazards specific to this trade.</p>
<p><strong>Respiratory sensitization claims</strong> — unlike a cut or a broken bone, isocyanate sensitization can take time to manifest. A worker who develops asthma after months of exposure may not make the connection immediately. These delayed claims require a workers' comp policy that recognizes occupational disease exposure in spray foam environments.</p>
<p><strong>Eye injuries</strong> — chemical splash from spray guns and foam equipment.</p>
<p><strong>Heat stress</strong> — applicators working in attics during summer months face significant heat exposure, especially while wearing full PPE.</p>
<h2>Class Codes for Spray Foam Workers</h2>
<p>Workers' compensation class codes determine how your payroll is rated — different codes carry different rates based on the injury risk of the work. For spray foam contractors, the key codes are:</p>
<p><strong>Class Code 5473 — Insulation Work</strong></p>
<p>This is the most commonly used code for spray foam applicators, and it's the right starting point for insulation work. Code 5473 covers the installation of thermal insulation in buildings, including spray foam. The rate under 5473 reflects insulation-specific hazards including fall exposure and some chemical hazard.</p>
<p>However, 5473 is often applied too broadly. Helpers, material handlers, and office staff shouldn't be coded 5473.</p>
<p><strong>Class Code 5474 — Insulation Work, Steam Pipes</strong></p>
<p>This applies to industrial insulation around pipes and equipment — a different work environment than residential or commercial spray foam. Using 5474 for standard spray foam work is a misclassification.</p>
<p><strong>Class Code 8810 — Clerical Office Employees</strong></p>
<p>Office staff, estimators working entirely in the office, and bookkeepers should be coded 8810, which carries a significantly lower rate. Lumping these employees under 5473 overstates payroll in the high-rate category.</p>
<p><strong>Supervisory vs. Applicator Classification</strong></p>
<p>Supervisors who primarily supervise rather than apply foam have a different exposure profile and may qualify for a supervisory or light-duty code in some states. The classification should reflect what the employee actually does.</p>
<h2>What Misclassification Looks Like in Practice</h2>
<p><strong>Scenario 1: All employees under one code</strong></p>
<p>A spray foam contractor has three applicators, one helper, and one office manager. The broker puts everyone under 5473 at the same rate. The result: the office manager's salary is rated at the insulation worker rate — significantly overpaying on that portion of payroll. At audit, some carriers will correct this; others won't if you don't flag it.</p>
<p><strong>Scenario 2: Wrong code for the work type</strong></p>
<p>A contractor doing primarily roof foam (spray polyurethane foam roofing systems) gets coded under standard insulation codes instead of roofing codes. The roofing exposure — elevated work, specialized fall risk — may not be correctly rated, creating a mismatch between the rate paid and the actual risk profile.</p>
<p><strong>Scenario 3: Chemical exposure not recognized</strong></p>
<p>A generic insulation code may not specifically recognize isocyanate or chemical exposure as an occupational disease hazard for that code. When a worker files a chemical exposure claim, the carrier may dispute the claim's relationship to the classification. A broker who documents the chemical exposure component of the work up front creates a cleaner path for those claims.</p>
<h2>The Audit Problem</h2>
<p>Workers' comp premiums are based on estimated payroll at policy inception, then audited at the end of the year against actual payroll. Misclassification creates two audit problems:</p>
<p><strong>Under-classification</strong> — if your applicators are coded under a rate that doesn't reflect spray foam exposure, you may be underpaying. At audit, the carrier can reclassify and bill you for the difference — sometimes a significant amount if the audit covers a full year.</p>
<p><strong>Over-classification</strong> — if your office staff is coded as applicators, you're overpaying all year and not getting a refund unless you catch it.</p>
<p>The cleanest audit is one where the classification was right at inception, the payroll was split correctly across codes, and the actual work matches what the underwriter was told. We document this at the start of every policy.</p>
<h2>1099 Subcontractors and Workers' Comp Exposure</h2>
<p>Spray foam contractors who use 1099 subcontractors face a significant workers' comp exposure that many don't account for.</p>
<p>In most states, if a subcontractor doesn't carry their own workers' comp, they're deemed an employee for purposes of your policy — meaning an injury to that 1099 sub becomes your claim.</p>
<p>The audit will also include uninsured subcontractor payments in your payroll calculation unless you can document:</p>
<ul>
<li>Their certificate of insurance showing active workers' comp</li>
<li>The effective dates covering the work they performed</li>
</ul>
<p>We help clients set up a subcontractor documentation system so they're not caught at audit with a large bill for subs who weren't properly covered.</p>
<h2>How to Review Your Current Program</h2>
<p>Pull out your workers' comp declarations page and look at:</p>
<ol>
<li><strong>What codes appear for your employees</strong> — do they reflect the actual jobs each person does?</li>
<li><strong>Whether your applicators are coded 5473</strong> (or the appropriate code for your state)</li>
<li><strong>Whether office staff are separated out</strong> from field workers</li>
<li><strong>Whether your loss runs are attached</strong> — prior claims history should be submitted to underwriters so the rate reflects your actual experience, not the worst-case assumption</li>
</ol>
<p>If you're unsure about any of this, bring your declarations page and payroll breakdown to a broker who understands spray foam. A 15-minute review can identify premium overpayments and coverage gaps before your next renewal.</p>
<h2>What Correct Coverage Looks Like</h2>
<p>The right workers' comp program for a spray foam contractor:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Codes that match actual job functions</strong> — applicators, helpers, supervisors, and office staff each coded correctly</li>
<li><strong>Experience modification factor applied</strong> — if you have 3+ years of history, your EMR should be used; don't let a carrier rate you at standard if your experience is better</li>
<li><strong>Chemical exposure documented</strong> — so that occupational disease claims from isocyanate exposure aren't disputed at the classification level</li>
<li><strong>Subcontractor documentation process in place</strong> — certificates collected and filed before audit season</li>
<li><strong>Employers' liability limits adequate</strong> — the Part Two limits on your workers' comp should be high enough to handle an employer negligence claim if one is ever filed</li>
</ul>
